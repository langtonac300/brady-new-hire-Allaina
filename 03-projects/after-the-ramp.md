# After the ramp — what year one looks like

Day 90 is the end of the ramp, not the end of the plan. This is the honest preview of what
comes next.

> **Nothing here is assigned.** It's the map of what's genuinely available, so you can start
> forming opinions about what you want. The actual choosing happens with Alex, and it should
> be a conversation you drive.

---

## The first thing that changes: how work reaches you

For 90 days, work arrives as a ladder — numbered projects in a sensible order, each one
sized for you. **That stops.** After the ramp, work reaches you through two mechanisms
instead, and they're both quarterly or annual rather than weekly.

### Rocks — the quarterly one

A **rock** is a commitment to finish something meaningful inside about 90 days. One to three
per person, due-dated, reviewed out loud every Tuesday in the huddle
([`../02-learning/the-l10-huddle.md`](../02-learning/the-l10-huddle.md)). They're set in a
working session at each fiscal-quarter turn.

Your first rock is the real marker that the ramp is over. It's also the first time you'll be
asked *"what should you be working on?"* rather than told.

### Goals — the annual one

Brady runs formal goals: **three to five per person, including one personal goal**, set for
the fiscal year. They're written as outcomes, not activities — the result, with the activity
as its leading indicator.

Both rocks and goals get tagged to the team's **four strategic shifts**, so it's worth
knowing what they are.

---

## The four shifts

Alex's framing for where paid media is going. Everything on this team eventually ladders up
to one of them.

| | The shift | What it means in practice |
|---|-----------|--------------------------|
| **1** | **Agentic PPC is real** | The recurring loops — search terms, negatives, hygiene checks — get run by automation, with a person auditing rather than clicking. Your daily keep/kill review is the manual version of this |
| **2** | **Measurement past last-click** | First-party data, media-mix modeling, incrementality testing. Moving from "what did the platform claim" to "what actually happened because of us" |
| **3** | **AI-search visibility and on-site behavior** | How the brand shows up in AI answers, and what people actually do once they land. Watch-and-learn stage — genuinely open ground |
| **4** | **Automation needs structure and a page that converts** | Feed segmentation, product data quality, and landing-page conversion work. The machine can only optimize what you've structured for it |

**Shift 3 is the one with the most room in it**, and the least existing expertise to compete
with. Worth knowing if you like being early on something.

## Depth — finishing what the ramp deliberately left partial

Three things the 90 days on purpose don't complete:

| | Where it stands at day 90 | What year one looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **The Wristbands stack** | Partial understanding, and that's the stated expectation — it runs on different technology from everything else and it's the steepest curve you have | Full independence is more like a six-month target. Getting there is a real, nameable goal |
| **The conversion-tracking work** | You'll have diagnosed and documented a config problem; someone senior executes the fix | Owning the follow-through — verifying it did what you said it would, then finding the next one. Diagnosing your second one is much faster than your first |
| **Mecco** | Yours, and fixed — the defects found and repaired | The shift from *fixing* an account to *growing* one. Different skill, and a much better story to tell about your year |

## Breadth — the tools the ramp only exposed you to

Your ramp trains some tools properly and only introduces others. That's a deliberate trade,
and the honest version is that at day 90 there'll be a list of things you've touched once.

The ones most worth converting from "used it once" to "can operate it unaided":

- **BigQuery.** The biggest single unlock. It's where the numbers the business actually
  reports live, and being able to write your own pull rather than request one changes what
  questions you can ask.
- **The conversion tracking layer (GTM).** Once you can read it, a whole class of "why
  doesn't this number match" questions become answerable by you.
- **Microsoft/Bing Ads**, which is genuinely different from Google in ways that matter.

And there's a set the ramp doesn't cover at all — other channels the team runs. **Say that
list out loud with Alex at day 90.** The failure mode is that nobody names it, and by month
four everyone assumes ninety days means you know everything.

## Directions that are actually open

Real work that exists, needs someone, and would be a credible rock. Ask Alex which of these
is live before you get attached to one — priorities move.

**Landing pages and conversion.** Page speed against conversion rate, and how differently
desktop and mobile behave. This has been flagged internally as high-value and largely
unexamined. It's Shift 4, it's measurable, and it's the kind of work that produces a number
you can defend.

**Waste at scale.** The daily keep/kill review catches waste one term at a time. The
program-level version asks where spend goes on search terms that never had a chance, across
a whole account group, and what structural change stops it recurring.

**Quality and serving hygiene.** Quality Score distribution, ad strength against what ads
actually do, and disapproved ads quietly not serving. Unglamorous, and it's usually where the
easy money is.

**Extending the negatives loop.** The automated search-terms loop runs on some account groups
and not others. Ask Alex where the gaps are — taking a loop that works and extending it to an
account that doesn't have it is one of the highest-leverage things a second-year analyst can do.

**Feed and product data.** What gets sent about products, how it's labeled, and what breaks
downstream when the labels are wrong or missing. Shift 4, and it connects paid search to a
lot of other teams.

**Experiments as a program.** Running your first test is a ramp project. Owning a testing
roadmap — a backlog, a prioritization, a rhythm of reading results out and killing what
lost — is a year-one thing.

## The scale shift

The ladder taught you to work at increasing sizes on purpose: an hour, half a day, two days,
a week. Year one continues that curve.

```
   1 hour  →  half day  →  1–2 days  →  1 week  →  a quarter (a rock)  →  a program
   ├─────────── the 90-day ramp ───────────┤   ├──────── year one ────────┤
```

**A program is the real destination.** It's work with no end date — the negatives loop, the
experiment roadmap, the feed quality effort — that runs because you run it, and that somebody
would notice stopping. That's what "owns it" means at the next level up.

---

## Being seen

Doing good work and nobody knowing is a real failure mode, and it's avoidable here.

1. **Own your Deep Dive section**, monthly, with the mechanism behind every number. That's
   the Day-90 target and it stays your most reliable shop window.
2. **Present to the wider digital team** when you have something worth presenting — Alex
   carries items up from the huddle, and eventually you carry your own.
3. **Ask to go to a conference.** The team has done it before and there's an established
   shape for the ask: what it costs, what you'd attend, what you'd learn, how you'd apply it
   here, and a readout afterward. **Asks framed in terms of what the business gets tend to
   land; asks framed as "I'd like to go" tend not to.** That's not cynicism, it's just the
   register.

---

## One piece of advice about all of this

**Don't try to pick from this list at day 91.** You'll pick badly, because the thing that
looks most interesting from here is rarely the thing you turn out to be good at.

Instead: notice what you keep getting pulled into. The problems you find yourself thinking
about in the shower are better evidence than any plan. Then bring *that* to Alex as a
recommendation, with a reason — which, conveniently, is the exact skill the whole 90 days was
training.

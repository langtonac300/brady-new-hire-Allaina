## The point

T3-3 asked where the *next increment* of budget should go on PDC — an account with years of
history, an established target and a guardrail with a track record behind it. You had something
to read against.

**Mecco has none of that.** No prior year, no proven cost per lead, no efficiency target with
evidence under it. So this is the harder version of the same question, and it's the version you'll
meet far more often in a career than the T3-3 version:

> **How much should a program spend before anyone knows what a lead costs?**

And there's a second half specific to this account: most of it isn't running. So the
recommendation isn't only *how much* — it's **which campaigns get turned on, in what order, and
why those first.**

---

## What to do

### 1 · Say what a lead costs — with the caveat, or say you can't yet

From T4-1. Two things will complicate it, and handling them correctly is most of the grade:

- **The value change from [M-2](./m-02-fix-the-conversion-values.md) sits inside your history.**
  Cost per conversion either side of that date is measured with two different rulers. Say the date.
- **You may not have enough conversions to have a number at all.**

⚠️ **On the second one: a cost per lead built from a handful of conversions is not a benchmark,
it's an anecdote with a decimal point.** *"We don't have a defensible cost per lead yet — here's
what we do have, here's the volume we'd need, and here's roughly when we'd have it"* is a
legitimate answer and a more useful one than a confident number that's mostly noise. This is the
cold-start rule: **the first stretch of a new program is learning, not benchmarking.** If someone
senior asks you for a benchmark from this data, that's the answer — plus what you'd need and when.

### 2 · Find out what's paused, and why

Count what exists, what's enabled, and what each campaign's daily budget is set to. Then find out
**why** the paused ones are paused. Some of those reasons will still be valid and some won't —
"it was never turned on" and "it was turned off because it was buying junk" lead to completely
different recommendations.

### 3 · Do the multiplication

Take the daily budget on a single campaign, multiply by the number of campaigns, multiply by 30.

Compare that to what the program is actually meant to spend in a month.

⚠️ **If those two numbers are wildly different, the per-campaign figure is a placeholder that only
works while most of the account is paused** — and un-pausing the rest without changing it would
commit the program to a number nobody agreed to. Catching that before it happens is squarely this
project's job. Say it plainly if you find it.

### 4 · Recommend a sequence, not a switch

Which campaigns come on first, and why those. The instinct to argue with is "turn them all on and
see" — that spends the learning budget on everything at once and teaches you nothing about any of
it.

Two questions per candidate, adapted from T3-3:

| Question | What a "yes" means |
|---|---|
| Is there **demand it can't currently serve** — is it capped, or losing impression share to budget? | Money here buys clicks you're already missing. The cleanest case there is |
| Is this a theme you have **no data on at all**? | Money here buys *information*. Legitimate on a cold start — but call it what it is, and size it accordingly |

Those are two different purchases and they justify different amounts. Be explicit about which one
each recommended campaign is.

### 5 · Write the one page

Where the money goes, how much, in what order, and the arithmetic behind it. **One clear
recommendation** — defensible to someone who'll push back — not a menu.

---

## ⚠️ A guardrail is a ceiling, not a target — but check one exists first

The T3-3 instinct still holds: when pacing is behind and efficiency is inside the guardrail, the
move is usually to **push spend, not cut it.** Most new analysts reach for "cut to protect
efficiency," and it's usually wrong.

**The twist here is that this account may not have a guardrail yet.** If nobody has set an
efficiency target for Mecco, that's a finding to raise with Alex — not a gap to paper over by
picking a number that sounds reasonable. An invented target becomes the number everyone quotes
six months later, and by then nobody remembers you made it up.

## ⚠️ Don't recommend a move that a test should decide

Straight from T3-3, and it applies harder here. *"Turn on campaign X at $Y/day"* is a
recommendation. *"This will produce leads at $Z"* is a **prediction**, and on an account with no
history it's a guess wearing a number.

Where the money is obvious — unserved demand, inside whatever guardrail exists — recommend the
move. Where it depends on how a campaign *responds*, say it should be measured, and say what
you'd need to see to call it.

## The point

Two accounts. Same manager account, same company, same kind of buyer, overlapping product
ranges. **Their platform ROAS numbers are wildly different.**

That gap is the puzzle. Some of it is a real difference in how the two accounts make money.
Some of it is the two accounts *counting differently* — and telling those apart is the
single most transferable skill on this ramp. Once you've done it here, you'll recognize the
shape everywhere.

**This is the clearest predict-then-compare project in Tier 1. Follow the steps in order —
the order is the exercise.**

---

## What to do

### Step 1 — get the numbers

Pull platform ROAS for **Seton US** and for **EMEDCO** over the same window. Write both
down, with the window.

### Step 2 — write your hypothesis. Before checking anything.

The obvious lever is **channel mix**: EMEDCO is Shopping-first, Seton runs Search and
Shopping at comparable scale. Shopping and Search have different economics, so some of the
gap should come from there.

**Write down how much of the gap you think that explains, and why.** A number or a fraction,
not a vibe. This step is worthless if you skip it or do it after step 3, and it will be
tempting to do both.

### Step 3 — now go and check

Open each account's **conversion actions** and compare the two baskets:

- Which actions are flagged **primary** on each account?
- What **value** does each one carry?
- Are they all purchases? Or are some of them leads carrying an assigned value — a quote
  request, a catalog request, a phone call?
- Is anything being imported from outside the platform?

### Step 4 — write the answer

How much of the gap is a real efficiency difference between two businesses, and how much is
the two accounts counting different things and calling both of them "revenue"?

Then: **how far off was your hypothesis, and what did you not know when you wrote it?**

---

## 🛑 Stop here until your step-2 hypothesis is written down

**The section below gives away a large part of the answer.** That's unavoidable — it's also
a safety rule you need before you quote either of these numbers anywhere.

So: **write your hypothesis first, save it, then read on.** If you've already read ahead,
say so in your write-up rather than pretending otherwise — an honest "I read the warning
first and it steered me" is worth more than a hypothesis you've quietly back-fitted.

## ⚠️ Platform ROAS on this account group is not a revenue number

These two accounts bid on a blended mix that includes actual purchases, **placeholder values
assigned to leads**, and an offline-value import. That mix inflates and distorts platform
ROAS well beyond the usual first-touch-versus-platform gap.

**Use it to compare campaigns against each other inside one account. Never report it as
revenue.** For real revenue, go through the first-touch path like everywhere else.

---

## What good looks like

- Your hypothesis is written down, dated, and demonstrably from before step 3.
- Your answer names **at least one specific conversion action** that inflates one side.
  "The conversion basket is messy" is the observation; naming the action is the finding.
- You can state what you would need in order to compare these two accounts *fairly* — and
  whether platform data alone can get you there.
- You noticed how easy it would have been to quote the higher number in a meeting as
  straightforward evidence that one account is better run than the other.

## The point

A **keyword** is what you bid on. A **search term** is what a person actually typed. They
are not the same thing, and the space between them is where wasted spend lives.

This is the mechanical foundation under the daily search-terms review you'll be running for
the rest of your time here. Doing it slowly once, on 30 real rows, is worth more than
reading about match types three times.

---

## What to do

1. Open the **search terms report** on PDC Healthcare, last 30 days.
2. **Take 30 terms — but not the top 30 by spend.** Take a mix: some high-cost, some
   one-click, some that look obviously right, some that look odd. The tail is where the
   surprises are.
3. For each row, record four things:

   | Search term | Keyword that triggered it | Match type | Expected? |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | what they typed | what you're bidding on | broad / phrase / exact | yes/no + one line |

4. **Then write three lines at the bottom:** the match you found most surprising, the
   mechanism that produced it, and whether you'd have predicted it.

---

## ⚠️ This is not a Keep/Kill exercise

You're not deciding what to block. That's the daily search-terms review and T2-1 later in
the ladder, and it asks a different question entirely (*could someone typing this ever be
shopping for what we sell?*).

Here the only question is: **did this keyword matching this search surprise me?** Resist the
urge to start building a negatives list — you'll do that properly later, with more context.

---

## What good looks like

- You can point at a specific broad-match keyword pulling in something you'd never have
  predicted, and explain the **mechanism** — how Google got from that keyword to that
  search.
- "Broad match is loose" is not the answer. *Why* it went where it went is.
- Your 30 rows include at least a few one-click terms. If every row is a top spender, you
  sampled the easy part.

**Common first-time trap:** assuming an exact-match keyword can only ever pull the exact
phrase. Look for a close variant in your 30 and you'll see why that assumption costs money.

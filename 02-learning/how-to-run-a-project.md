# How to run a project

Every brief tells you *what to do*. This is the part around it — what to sort out before you
start, and what happens once the deliverable exists.

**Read this once. After that it's about five minutes of habit per project.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Before** | Write your prediction. Check the scope. Get your screens ready. Pick the right time |
| **During** | Work the brief. Treat the timebox as data |
| **After** | Write it up the same day → readout → file the loose ends → log what you got wrong |

---

## Before

### 1 · Write down what you think the answer is — *before you open anything*

This is the one that actually matters, and it only works in that order.

Most briefs are built on **predict-then-compare**: you make your call, then you see the
answer, then you explain the difference. The learning is entirely in that third step.

**But a prediction written after you've peeked isn't a prediction.** You'll unconsciously
adjust it, decide you basically knew, and skip the only part that teaches anything. That's
not a discipline problem, it's how memory works — everyone does it unless the prediction is
already written down.

So: read the brief, close it, and write two or three lines in your project file first.

> *"I think PDC Healthcare will be mostly search with one PMax. I'd guess brand is a small
> share of spend. I have no idea what it optimizes to."*

Thirty seconds. **"I have no idea" is a legitimate and useful prediction** — it makes the
gap visible later, which is the whole point.

### 2 · Check what you're *not* doing

Most briefs have a boundary in them and it's easy to blow past. T1-1 tells you to notice
what each campaign optimizes to **and move on** — the proper investigation is T1-4. Going
deep there feels productive and turns a one-hour project into three.

**If the brief doesn't say where the edge is, ask before you start**, not at hour two.

### 3 · Get your screens ready

Sounds trivial; it's twenty minutes every time it isn't done. Before you start the clock:
the right account open, the right date range set, the right report found, and your project
file already created in `04-my-work/projects/`.

### 4 · Pick the right time of day

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Don't ship a live change late on a Friday** | If it does something unexpected, you want people around. This is a normal professional habit, not a rule about you |
| **Don't ship one right before you're out** | Same reason. You should be there for the day after |
| **Do the analysis work when you're fresh** | The keep/kill and inventory projects are attention work. They go badly at 4:30pm |

## What a paired session is actually like

Nobody tells new people this, so here it is.

**Alex will let you run.** He isn't waiting to catch you out, and being wrong in a paired
session costs nothing — that's precisely why the projects where a wrong mental model is
expensive to unlearn are the paired ones.

He'll interrupt if you're heading somewhere that would waste real time or money. Otherwise
he'll mostly let you get there yourself, because that's what makes it stick.

**Your job in a paired session is to think out loud.** Silence looks like understanding and
isn't. Say the half-formed version — "I think this is the brand one because…" — even when
you're not sure. Especially then.

**Ask at least two questions.** If you got through an hour inside an account you'd never
seen without a question, you were performing rather than learning.

## After

### 1 · Write it up the same day

Not because of a deadline — because **you will lose the reasoning by tomorrow.** You'll keep
the conclusion and lose why you believed it, and the why is the part worth having.

If the day gets away from you, write five bullets before you log off and finish it in the
morning. Five bullets same-day beats a polished page two days later.

### 2 · The readout: three parts, ten minutes

Several projects end with a short readout. There's a version of this that wastes everyone's
time, and it's the version almost everyone does first:

🔴 **The common one:** a narration of what you did. *"I opened the account, I filtered to 30
days, I sorted by cost, then I looked at each campaign…"* Nobody needs the walkthrough.

🟢 **The useful one — three parts:**

| Part | One line each |
|---|---|
| **What I found** | The conclusion, first. Not the method |
| **What I was wrong about** | Your prediction vs. what was actually there, and what caused the gap |
| **What I'd do next** | One thing. Even if it's "I'd want to check X" |

That middle part is the one people leave out, and it's the one worth hearing. **A readout
with no surprise in it usually means the project was too easy or you didn't look hard
enough.**

### 3 · File the loose ends — don't leave them in the document

Every project turns up things you noticed but didn't chase. Right now they die in your
write-up. Give each one a destination:

| What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Something you didn't understand | `04-my-work/questions/` |
| Something that looks broken | Tell Alex. Don't sit on it because you're not certain — "this looks wrong, I might be misreading it" is a complete and welcome sentence |
| Something worth doing later | Say it in your readout so it can become a real to-do with an owner |
| Something worth testing | Same — test-shaped ideas have their own home, and Alex will route it |

### 4 · Log what you got wrong

`04-my-work/what-i-got-wrong/` — one running file, one line per surprise, all 90 days.

**This is the highest-value thing you'll produce during the ramp**, and it takes about a
minute per project. Two reasons:

- **Patterns show up that no single project reveals.** Four entries in, you'll notice you
  consistently assume the platform number is the real number, or that you keep expecting
  brand and non-brand to behave the same way. That's a fixable habit — but only once you can
  see it.
- **It's the evidence for your Day-90 re-score.** When you fill in
  [`baseline.md`](../05-self-assessment/baseline.md) again, this file is what stops the
  exercise being a vague feeling about whether you improved.

**Nobody grades it and nobody else has to read it.** Write it for yourself and be blunt.

### 5 · Say whether the timebox held

If a one-hour project took three, **say so.** That's a scoping error on our side and it's
useful information — the ladder gets fixed with that feedback and stays wrong without it.

The same goes the other way: if a half-day project took forty minutes, that's worth knowing
too.

## What happens to your deliverable

**Alex reads it. It isn't graded, and it doesn't go into any review.** It sits in your folder
as a record of your own thinking, and the genuinely useful ones get reused — a good structure
map is the thing the next person reads instead of starting cold.

That's the actual reason every project has a deliverable. Not to prove you did the work — to
leave something behind.
